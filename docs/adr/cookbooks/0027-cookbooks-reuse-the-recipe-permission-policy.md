# Cookbooks reuse the recipe permission policy

Norish is household-first, and the obvious shape for a shared cookbook is a household-owned entity. **A Cookbook instead belongs to its Owner, as a recipe does, and is seen, edited and deleted under the instance's one Permission Policy (`RECIPE_PERMISSION_POLICY`, ADR-0041).** No new administrator setting, no second permission rule to hold in mind, and no case where a cookbook is visible under rules its recipes are not. On a default instance — `view: everyone`, `edit: household`, `delete: household` — that means cookbooks are as browsable across households as the recipes are. A self-hoster who wants them private has the switch already; it simply also applies to recipes, which is the point of there being one switch.

Membership is the other half, and it is the half that looks wrong. **Filing a recipe into a cookbook requires view on the recipe and edit on the cookbook, and never writes the recipe row** — its version is not bumped, its Owner is not notified, nothing about it changes. So under the default policy a person can collect a recipe they could not edit, which reads as a permission bug until you know it was chosen. Membership is a fact about the cookbook rather than about the recipe, and the alternative is worse than untidy: requiring edit rights on both would leave a reader on a default instance browsing hundreds of recipes and able to file almost none of them.

## Considered Options

- **Household-owned cookbooks**, kept by the household when their maker leaves. Truest to the product's framing and rejected for the second rule it introduces: a member who leaves takes their recipes with them (ADR-0041), so their cookbooks would stay behind under a rule their recipes do not follow.
- **Always-personal cookbooks**, visible only to their creator whatever the policy says. Makes "Your cookbooks" literally true, and makes it impossible for a household to build one together, which is the main thing a household would want a cookbook for.
- **Deriving cookbook visibility from the policy's `edit` level** rather than its `view` level, which would make cookbooks household-scoped by default while staying administrator-tunable without a new setting. Rejected as a coupling nobody would predict: who may see a cookbook would follow a setting named for editing, so showing cookbooks beyond the household would also let every other household rename them.
- **Requiring edit rights on the recipe** to file it. Never surprises a recipe's Owner, at the cost of making the feature nearly inert under the shipped default.

## Consequences

- Because a cookbook is exactly as visible as the recipes its Owner could see, a cookbook holding a member its viewer cannot see does not arise while the policy and its Owner's household stay fixed: with `everyone` nothing is filtered, and with `household` the audience for the cookbook and the audience for its members are the same set. It arises only when a cookbook holds another household's recipe, filed while the policy allowed it or carried along when its Owner changed household, and the policy forbids reading across; those members are hidden while it does and come back when it widens (ADR-0041). Member queries reuse the same policy condition the recipes list applies, so the count and the list agree by construction and the rare case degrades quietly rather than showing a locked placeholder or a count that does not match what is on screen.
- Two readers can honestly see different member counts for the same cookbook. That is the Library rule applied one level down, not a bug to reconcile.
- Deleting a cookbook never touches its recipes. Deleting a recipe removes it from every cookbook it was in, and may leave cookbooks empty, which is an ordinary state. Deleting an account erases the cookbooks it owns along with its recipes.
- A recipe's Owner has no say over which cookbooks it appears in and no view of them. Giving them one would mean notifying on membership, which turns a private act of filing into a social one.
- Giving cookbooks their own level in the Permission Policy, or hard-scoping their visibility to the household regardless of the policy, reopens this.
